Direction D3

Optical alignment audit

Reading panel may be mathematically centered but typography reads left-heavy. Compare four variants below.

Diagnosis

  • Panel padding — Current production uses equal horizontal padding (3rem). The white box is symmetric; the text block is not optically centered because content is full-bleed within the panel.
  • Reading measure — No max-width on body copy. Long lines anchor hard to the left edge; ragged right creates uneven white space that reads as mass shifted left.
  • Headline width — Serif display at 2rem spans nearly the full panel. Left vertical stems (W, h, l) add visual weight on the gutter side.
  • Column alignment — Takeaway bullets use padding-left, pulling list mass left. Meta and breadcrumb are left-flush while the sidebar sits outside the panel, compounding page-level asymmetry.
  • Optical centering — Mathematical center ≠ perceived center for serif headlines + indented lists. Premium publications center a measure, not a container.

What does anxiety actually feel like?

Anxiety · Kenneth W. Christian, PhD · June 2026 · Ref ANX-042

Anxiety is not only worry in your thoughts. For many people it shows up in the body first — tight chest, restlessness, difficulty sleeping — before they have language for what is happening.

Key takeaways

  • Physical symptoms (racing heart, shallow breathing, muscle tension) are common and real.
  • Anxiety and stress overlap, but anxiety often persists after the immediate trigger passes.
  • Naming the sensation can reduce fear of the sensation itself — a core loop in many anxiety patterns.
  • When symptoms interfere with sleep, work, or relationships for weeks, professional evaluation helps.

What you might be experiencing

People often describe a sense of impending danger without a clear source. Your nervous system may feel switched on — hypervigilant, irritable, or unable to settle. Some notice gastrointestinal discomfort, headaches, or tingling. Others describe emotional numbness alternating with sudden fear.

What can help

Grounding in the body (slow breathing, orienting to the room, reducing caffeine late in the day) can lower acute activation. Structured routines around sleep and movement often stabilize baseline arousal. Therapy approaches such as CBT can address the thought patterns that maintain anxiety over time.
